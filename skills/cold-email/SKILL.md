---
name: cold-email
description: Write cold emails that get replies from busy people. Use when drafting outreach to investors, advisors, founders, or any busy contact.
---

Busy people skim. Assume you have ten seconds. Cut the email to a few sentences, link to more information for the reader who has time, and put contact details in the signature. Assume they will not open links or attachments, so the email must stand alone.

Make the subject concrete. "Seeking fundraising advice for FlightCaster (intro from John Smith)" beats "Re: fundraising advice". "Quick question about your talk at Config 2024" beats "Loved your talk".

Open with one line: shared context (where you met, or their specific work) and what you do. Then why you are writing, then the ask.

Make the ask explicit. Want a meeting? Name the topic, a length, and 2-3 times. Want an intro? Ask, and attach a self-contained blurb they can forward. Fundraising? Say so and ask to show the pitch. Skip Calendly when you ask for their time; the link puts the scheduling work on them.

Give before you get. Offer genuine enthusiasm for their work, a relevant insight, or a connection.

Check the email against four tests before you send. It is short, because anything over two paragraphs lands in the "later" bucket and later never comes. It is clear who you are, through past roles or a body of work (Twitter, a paper, a repo, a blog); use the second until you can use the first. It has a value proposition for the receiver, like ideas for their company or a thoughtful response to something they wrote. It has one specific, low-friction ask they can do in seconds: "follow me on Twitter", "here is a live demo account", "a PDF of screenshots". "Help me with advice" and "thoughts on my startup?" go to the todo bucket and die there.

Do not write your name at the start; they see it in the From field. Do not repeat yourself. Proofread; typos kill credibility. All lowercase for vibes. Avoid jargon.

Break these rules often.
